History of the city of Scupi

Ancient Scupi in Roman times (2nd century BC to 4th century AD) was one of the most important cities in the central and eastern Balkans.

Its remains lie about five kilometres north-west of Skopje, on the left bank of the Vardar, at the foot of the southern slope of the Zajčev Rid hill.

At the end of the 1st century BC the Romans conquered Scupi and its surroundings and began Romanising the native population by settling veterans from their legions. The largest settlement of veterans, from the 7th Claudian Legion, came under the emperor Domitian (81-96 AD), of the Flavian dynasty (69-96). Scupi became a deductive colony, which laid the basis for the city's extraordinary growth and prosperity.

Thanks to its geographical position, as an important crossroads linking the Aegean with the Danube region and Central Europe, Scupi became one of the main economic, administrative, religious and cultural centres of Macedonia. Historical sources show that after the Edict of Milan of 313 Scupi became the seat of a bishopric. The bishop of Scupi, Dacus, took part in the First Ecumenical Council at Nicaea in 325. In 458 Ursillus, bishop of Dalmatia, is mentioned, and the names of two more bishops, Paregorius and Ursinus, are recorded. The Roman emperor Theodosius I stayed in Scupi twice (379 and 388). Scupi flourished most from the 1st to the 4th century, which made it a frequent target of barbarian tribes in late antiquity. Ancient Scupi was completely destroyed in the catastrophic earthquake of 518. But much of the population survived, as it had fled before the raids of the Slavs from beyond the Danube.

After this catastrophic earthquake the people of Scupi settled in the wider area, on the site of today's remains of the Skopje fortress - Kale.

The earthquake of 518

Record by Count Marcellinus:

"...Scupi lies at the confluence of the Lepenec and the Vardar. At that moment 24 fortresses collapsed. Two of them were laid waste together with their inhabitants, in four others half the buildings and inhabitants perished, in eleven a third, and in seven a quarter. The capital Scupi was razed to the ground."

He also writes that the earthquake was strong: the earth split open in several places and flames burst out of it as from a hot oven. One crack was thirty miles long and twenty feet wide, so that many people fleeing the disaster fell into it. The tremors brought down the trees too..

Justiniana Prima

In 535 Justinian I (527-565) founded the city of Justiniana Prima. Old records describe it as a city with beautiful buildings, streets and many fountains of drinking water. Sadly, they do not say where the city was, and its location has still not been confirmed by archaeology. It is thought that it stood on the site of today's Skopje, because Justinian I was born very near today's Skopje, in the village of Taor, so he founded Justiniana Prima as a monument to his birthplace. The fact that the Archbishopric of Justiniana Prima replaced the bishopric of Scupi supports a location in the Skopje region.

Justinian I, Byzantine emperor (527-565). On coming to the throne he sought to restore the former unity of the Roman Empire, whose capital was Constantinople. In economic policy he tried to preserve small and medium holdings, preventing large estates through laws. Justinian's legislation later served to develop Byzantine law, as well as that of several Balkan countries and of Europe more widely.

Samuel

Tsar Samuel (976-1014), the youngest of the four sons, the Cometopuli (young princes), of the prince Nicholas. In 969 Samuel and his three brothers (David, Moses and Aaron) rose up against Bulgarian rule, centred in the former Sklavinia "Berzitia" in south-western Macedonia. After Bulgarian rule was removed from this part of Macedonia, the foundations of the Macedonian state were laid, with the city of Prespa as its capital. Until 976 the state was ruled by the four brothers, a tetrarchy (rule of four).

In 971 Byzantium destroyed the First Bulgarian Empire and turned Bulgaria into a Byzantine province. But the newly created Macedonian state continued to exist, under the supreme authority of Byzantium.

In 976 the Cometopuli rose up against Byzantium, and Macedonia again became an independent state. The same year Samuel's three brothers were killed, and a monarchy headed by Samuel was established in Macedonia. As sole ruler he imposed his rule over the whole territory of Macedonia, except Thessaloniki, and over a large part of the Balkan Peninsula (Bulgaria, Serbia, Duklja, Bosnia, part of Albania, part of Dalmatia and part of Greece). He thus created all the conditions for the Macedonian state to become a powerful Balkan empire, named Samuel's empire after the founder of the dynasty. Samuel was proclaimed tsar in Prespa in the last years of the 10th century, receiving a crown from Pope Gregory V in Rome, who at the same time raised the Macedonian church to an archbishopric.

This, in turn, intensified Byzantine attacks on Samuel's empire. In 1004 the Byzantine emperor Basil II took Skopje through the betrayal of the governor Roman, and in the summer of 1014 he inflicted a catastrophic defeat on Samuel at the battle of Mount Belasica, where the prisoners of war were blinded, every hundredth man being left one eye to lead them back to their tsar Samuel. Samuel died soon after this defeat (6 October 1014).

Two years later Samuel's empire was also destroyed. (towards the end of 1018)

Description by the English traveller Dr Brown

"Skopje is a fine and lively place. The city has 700 tanners, who work hides in long, large troughs and take them out to sell. Skopje has several tombs and many fine houses, such as the house of the kadi and the house of the emir. The emir is of Muhammadan descent and is held in great respect in those parts. In the emir's courtyard there is a wonderful fountain, made to look like a fortress with towers around it, from whose tops rich jets of water spring. The rich houses are laid with precious carpets; the ceilings are decorated with triangular, square and other figures, gilded and painted with various ornaments. The city has a splendid bezistan, roofed with lead; many streets covered with planks; and many good merchants' houses in and around the city, all among trees and picturesque hills and little valleys. Skopje has many mosques. The finest stands on a high bank, with a wide porch in front resting on four marble columns. Right next to it is a wooden tower with a clock, from which there is a wonderful view of the city. There is also a vault that seems to be an ancient work, under which a small stream flows. Near the city is a magnificent aqueduct of about 200 arches, built of stone; it runs across a valley and is a splendid antiquity that adorns the area. When Muhammad I conquered Skopje, he settled it with many Asians, and so gave it the character of a Turkish city. Trade here is carried on with Belgrade, Thessaloniki and many other places."

Karpoš's Uprising

...After their defeat at Vienna, the Turks were forced to retreat. The defeat set off uprisings in the lands under Turkish rule, including Macedonia. The uprising was led by Karpoš. The insurgents freed the towns of Kratovo, Kumanovo and Kriva Palanka and reached Veles and Demir Kapija. They also appeared before Skopje and drove the Turks out of it. In those days the Turks were suffering defeats by the Austrian army, which under General Piccolomini was advancing through Kosovo and Kačanik towards Skopje. With the help of the Macedonian insurgents, the Austrians broke the last Turkish units at Kačanik, took the strongholds and marched on unhindered to Skopje. The Austrian troops reached liberated Skopje on 25 October 1689. Skopje delighted the Austrian soldiers. An Austrian officer wrote of it:

"Skopje covers a large area, but is somewhat smaller than Prague, or almost as big. Around the city there are many beautiful gardens on a large plain, unusually fertile and well cultivated. It has up to 60,000 inhabitants, including 3,000 Jews."

The Austrian commander, General Piccolomini, did not intend to go further south, but to break through Albania to Durrës. Before that he decided to destroy Skopje as a Turkish stronghold, to frighten the Turks and show the strength of his arms. So the next day he ordered Skopje to be burned.

After burning Skopje, the Austrian army, leaving its allies at the mercy of the Turkish army, withdrew towards the Kačanik gorge and Kosovo. On 4 November 1689 Piccolomini left Priština for Prizren, shaking with fever the whole way. He had caught the plague in Skopje, and on 9 November 1689 he died of the disease.

The Turks gathered their army near Adrianople, and the commanders held a council in Edirne. One of the main questions was how to crush the Macedonian insurgents led by Karpoš. The task fell to Halil Pasha. The Turks went on the offensive. The insurgents bravely defended first Kriva Palanka, then fought long and stubbornly at the fortress of Kumanovo. But there too they were defeated by the stronger army and massacred, and Karpoš was captured with his wife and two children.

The family was executed, and Karpoš was brought to Skopje, where, by the Stone Bridge, on 31 October 1689, he was impaled, then hung on the old plane tree as a warning to the people, and after a few days cut into pieces and thrown into the Vardar. The Turks retook Skopje and took bloody revenge on the whole Slav population, the Armenians and the Jews who had stayed behind and had not joined the great migration to the northern lands. Only those escaped the massacre by the Turks, who vented their fury at their defeats by the Austrian army on the innocent people of the Macedonian lands.

The burning of Skopje in 1689

Like Nero, Piccolomini, to the sound of military music, sat on Mount Vodno and watched Skopje burn. Piccolomini himself wrote about it to Leopold I: "The city of Skopje is almost as big as Prague, but without walls or ditches, and richly supplied with produce. The few people left in it walk the streets pale, full of fear and horror... Before we came it had 60,000 inhabitants. I decided, though it is not easy, to turn the city to ashes. I was sorry for the buildings, the like of which I have not seen in this war. Mosques of the finest marble and porphyry, with thousands of lamps, adorned with gilded Qurans that would draw attention even in Rome, fine antiquities, gardens and places of amusement, and, though to barbarian taste, great stores of food. All this I had to give to the flames. So as to leave the enemy nothing that could be saved, to strike fear and trembling into the barbarian peoples, and to make my lord's arms feared even in the most distant lands, I set about this deed. Men with torches were posted at every corner of the city. At the given signal (three cannon shots) the flames broke out. On 26 October 1689 smoke darkened the sun and the morning. We just stood on a high hill and, to the sound of military instruments, watched the burning of this beautiful place, truly without regret or sorrow, while my thoughts went back to the fact that the suburbs of Vienna had had to suffer the same fate."

Description of the city in 1839, by the botanist Grisebach

"The city really has no more than 2,000 houses, but most of them are remarkable and show the wealth of the citizens, especially those along the Vardar."

Of the Skopje region he says:

"The region stretches about 10 geographical miles from north to south and 30 miles from east to west. It has an area of about 300 square miles, with well-cultivated, densely populated plains. The climate is close to that of Lombardy, and wheat and rice grow very well. Silver-bearing lead ore is mined at Kratovo. Peace, order and security reign throughout the province, except in the land of the Albanians, who live on the Šar Mountain and in Debar; those places are only partly under the pasha's rule. The family of the pasha who governs this region has three brothers and is one of the richest families in the whole empire. The eldest, head of the family, is Amzi Pasha. Abduran Pasha lives in Tetovo, which he governs as his share. And Hasan Pasha commands the army, of which 2,000 soldiers were in Skopje and 1,000 in Tetovo."

Ilinden Uprising

On Ilinden, 2 August 1903, the Macedonian people began an organised armed uprising against Turkish rule. The uprising spread over the whole Bitola region, part of the Skopje and Thessaloniki regions, and later the Serres region. About 30,000 Macedonians took part. In the liberated territory, in the town of Kruševo, a revolutionary government was formed, and under the revolutionary Nikola Karev the Kruševo Republic was organised, which lasted 10 days. Although the Macedonian insurgents showed great courage and were ready for the ultimate sacrifice, after three months of fighting the uprising was crushed, and Kruševo and many Macedonian villages were burned.

The Ilinden Uprising won great sympathy among progressive people around the world. The progressive press followed not only the actions of the insurgents, but also the charities and committees that sent material and financial aid to Macedonia.
